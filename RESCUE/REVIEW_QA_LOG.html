HIV-2 Cross-Species Transfer Learning — Review Q&A Master Log

Every question raised during review, with the answer and where it is addressed.

Living document — update as new questions arrive. Paper: manuscript_corrected.html. Repo: github.com/nunnajaswant-ship-it/HIV-2_DRUG_RESISTANCE_TRANFER_LEARNING_ON_HIV-1.

Scope warning. A set of reviewer comments was recently supplied (gated fusion, HGFN, multimodal fusion, ESM-2 on HIV-1, fold-change thresholds, ECE calibration, DRV/TPV datasets). Those comments do not describe this manuscript. This paper has no gated fusion, no HGFN, no multimodal fusion, no fold-change classification and no ECE calibration; it is a cross-species transfer regression study (HIV-1 → HIV-2) using RandomForest/XGBoost on physicochemical descriptors. Items that genuinely apply are logged in Section C; items that belong to a different submission are listed in Section D and must not be applied here.

A. Questions raised during internal review

A1Do we have wet-lab results for HIV-1 and HIV-2?
Yes, for both. HIV-1: 5,683 measured Stanford HIVDB phenotypic assays (fold-resistance/IC50, 1,882 unique sequences). HIV-2: 321 measured ChEMBL assay rows (137 IC50; 160-row fine-tune panel) with real ChEMBL activity IDs. We did not run any experiments ourselves, and there is no prospective validation.
Where: §2.1 Datasets; Table 1; Data Availability.
A2Why these features? Relation to mutation and resistance?
Each residue is encoded by 21 physicochemical descriptors — Hellberg Z-scales (3), Kidera factors (10) and VHSE scales (8). They are properties of the amino acid, hence identical in both species, which is what makes transfer possible. Each family maps to a physical resistance mechanism: steric terms → clash with the inhibitor (I50V, I54M, I84V); hydrophobic terms → packing changes; electronic terms → loss of polar contact; structural-preference factors → flap flexibility.
Where: §2.3 Feature Engineering; Table 3 (descriptor→mechanism map).
A3Are the features sequence-only, structural, or both?
Sequence-only. They are derived entirely from the amino-acid sequence; no experimental structure enters the feature vector. Structural analysis (ONION-Net, PDB superposition) appears only as a separate explanatory analysis and as a comparator — and HIV-1-derived structural features are shown not to transfer (R = −0.14).
Where: §2.3; §4.4; Table 6.
A4What is the sequence-similarity cutoff, and the split ratio?
Cutoff: 95% identity. Sequences are clustered at 95% identity → 882 clusters + 714 singletons for HIV-1. Split: GroupKFold cross-validation using those clusters as groups, so no near-duplicate sequence is split across train and test. The HIV-2 fine-tune uses 5-fold out-of-fold pooling. The effective HIV-2 sample size is 19 unique sequences, not 160 rows.
Where: §2.2 step 6; §2.4; Table 5; §4.2.
A5How was redundancy removed, and why?
5,683 HIV-1 rows collapse to 1,882 unique sequences (same sequence measured against several drugs). Rows are de-duplicated per (sequence, drug); sequences are then clustered at 95% identity. Why: if near-identical sequences appeared in both train and test, the model would memorise isolates and inflate every correlation. Clustering is what makes the cross-validation honest.
Where: §2.2 step 6.
A6Why Random Forest and XGBoost? What happened with deep learning?
Sample size, not biological privilege. With 19 unique HIV-2 sequences, tree ensembles suit the regime: greedy feature selection over 2,121 descriptors, no scaling, reproducible from a seed, robust to uninformative inputs, and interpretable. Deep learning was tested and failed — PocketGNN R = −0.17, DBPT+ R = 0.21 — because 19 sequences cannot train a GNN. ESM-2 works only because it arrives pre-trained.
Where: §2.4; §4.5 limitation (h).
A7Which positions are masked, and what does masking mean?
Masking = selecting positions, not weighting them. Three nested sets, all from HIV-2 structure 3S45: core pocket, 16 residues within 5.0 Å — 8 23 25 27 28 29 30 31 32 47 48 49 50 81 82 84; Pocket-19 adds 26 45 46 54 76 80; extended 8.0 Å shell; and BASE-A = 16 core + 18 conserved neighbours = 34 residues. Masking does not improve over the global representation (0.5014–0.5036 vs 0.5163), so it is a mechanistic probe, not the deployment setting.
Where: §2.4 (pocket definitions and masking); Table 2.
A8What does the model predict — IC50 or something else?
Binding free energy ΔG in kcal/mol, not IC50. IC50 (nM) is converted thermodynamically: ΔG = RT·ln(IC50×10⁻⁹), RT = 0.593 kcal/mol at 298 K. For IC50 this is an approximation (Cheng-Prusoff correction unavailable without enzyme kinetics); for Ki/Kd it is exact.
Where: §2.2 step 5; §2.6 Sign Convention.
A9What did we do to the HIV-1 model to apply it to HIV-2 — weights or masking?
Neither weights nor masking — feature augmentation with a frozen model. (i) HIV-1 model trained then frozen; (ii) its ΔG prediction becomes a single appended meta-feature column; (iii) a new per-drug Random Forest is trained on [2,121-D | meta-feature]. No weights are modified, averaged or re-initialised.
Where: §2.4 (transfer protocol).
A10How different is the rule-based approach from our model?
The rule systems (Stanford penalty tables; HIV-2EU v4) are expert-curated lookup tables: they score only mutations they explicitly list, and return zero otherwise. We quantify this: the clinical benchmark is zero for 93.1% of isolates; the official HIV-2EU v4 rules leave 83.1% of the measured panel unscored, define no protease rules for 6 of 8 drugs, and cover 5 of 37 observed mutations. Our model is continuous, covers any substitution, and retains R = 0.585 on rows the rule leaves unranked.
Where: §3.6 head-to-head; §3.7 rule coverage; Table 9.
A11Is the transfer learning actually doing the work?
Not measurably, and we say so. The augmented model beats the same fine-tune without the meta-feature by 0.7018 vs 0.7015 (within noise), and a fine-tuning-size sweep (k = 1–30 rows/drug, 20 splits each) shows the HIV-1 contribution never exceeds 0.015 and does not grow as HIV-2 data is removed. HIV-2 data volume drives performance.
Where: §3.2; §4.5 limitation (p).
A12Is the headline number stable?
No — it is seed-sensitive and we report it. The locked R = 0.7018 uses one seed; across 10 seeds the benchmark is 0.628 ± 0.074 (range 0.516–0.715), so 0.7018 sits at the upper end. Comparisons are made only within a seed.
Where: §3.2; abstract; conclusion; §4.5 limitation (o).
A13Why does the pooled correlation look odd?
Pooling mixes within-drug signal with between-drug score scale, so pooled R is not an average of per-drug accuracy. Tested directly: raw pooled R = 0.701, and 0.653 after per-drug standardisation. Every clinical claim is therefore stated per drug.
Where: §3.2; §3.8; §4.5 limitation (d).
A14What is Mined_Expanded?
HIV-2 protease sequences retrieved from NCBI GenBank by automated Entrez query (db="protein", term="HIV-2 protease"), trimmed and aligned to the 99-residue frame, then assigned Stanford HIVDB penalty scores so they are comparable to the registry rows. 1,776 rows.
Where: §2.1 Datasets.
A15What is the sanity check that the HIV-1 and European datasets differ?
A rule engine reproducing the published Stanford HIV-2 penalty tables was applied to the clinical sequences and reproduced the HIVDB penalty exactly (R = 1.0000; 1,728/1,728 rows). That confirms (a) the rule engine is faithful and (b) the clinical target is a deterministic function of the mutation list — i.e. it is not an independent measurement, which is why the measured head-to-head is treated as primary.
Where: §3.6.
A16Zero-shot vs one-shot — what did we do?
Zero-shot (k = 0) and few-shot (k = 40 rows/drug, n = 160). Zero-shot: HIV-1 model applied directly to HIV-2, R = 0.5163, transfer ratio 0.699. Few-shot: fine-tune → R = 0.7018. No literal one-shot (k = 1) experiment is in the paper. Archived k = 1/5/10/20 runs exist only from the discarded deep-learning tiers and should not be quoted.
Where: §3.2; Table 4; Table 5.
A17Meta-learning — did we implement it?
No. What we use is stacked generalization (stacking): a level-2 "meta-model" fitted on level-1 out-of-fold predictions. Meta-learning is learning across tasks (MAML, prototypical networks) and is a different concept that shares the prefix. Our stack: base = Ridge, SVR, RandomForest, XGBoost, DANN; meta = Ridge(α = 5). The deployment model similarly uses the HIV-1 prediction as a single meta-feature. DANN is domain adaptation, not meta-learning.
Where: §2.4; Table 4 (ridge meta-ensemble).
A18Why is stacking needed at all?
Because no single base model dominates across all four drugs and all feature sets. Stacking lets a regularised linear meta-learner weight the base models per problem instead of committing to one. It was the strongest zero-shot configuration (R = 0.5818, vs 0.5587 XGBoost and 0.5163 global RF). The meta-learner is fitted on out-of-fold predictions so it cannot over-trust models on data they have seen.
Where: §3.2; Table 4.
A19Do we clash with any published work?
No. Literature sweep found no ML/DL resistance predictor for HIV-2 and no HIV-1→HIV-2 cross-species transfer for resistance. All ML resistance work found is HIV-1 only. Nearest same-corpus work (Farquhar 2026, Bioinformatics) uses the same Stanford PI dataset but stays within HIV-1.
Where: §1 Introduction; §4.1.

A2. Discussion points — full answers

D1What is the cutoff for sequence similarity?
Two cutoffs, serving different purposes.
(i) 95% identity — the clustering cutoff. Sequences are clustered at 95% identity, giving 882 clusters + 714 singletons for the HIV-1 corpus. This cutoff defines relatedness and is the grouping variable for cross-validation.
(ii) Exact 99-residue length — the framing filter. HIV protease is 99 residues; only exact-length sequences are retained, so every position is comparable across species.
HIV-2 diversity for context: identity of the ChEMBL panel to HIV-2 ROD wild-type is 88.9–99.0%, mean 95.0%, median 96.0%, and no sequence is 100% wild-type.
Why 95%: it is the standard HIV drug-resistance clustering threshold and it is conservative — it merges near-duplicates that would otherwise leak across folds.

D3Physicochemical features are specific to sequence only (not sequence + structure) — correct?
Yes, correct, and this is deliberate. The feature vector is built entirely from the amino-acid sequence: 21 descriptors per residue × 99 positions, plus summary statistics. No structural coordinate, contact map, or docking term enters the model input.
Why sequence-only is the right choice here: (a) no HIV-2 protease co-crystal structure exists for the four modelled inhibitors, so a structural feature would have to be modelled rather than measured; (b) descriptor values are properties of the amino acid and therefore identical in both species, which is what makes transfer meaningful; (c) empirically, HIV-1-derived structural features do not transfer (R = −0.1392, see D7) while sequence descriptors do.
Where: §2.3; §4.4; Table 7.
D4What sanity check established that the HIV-1 and the European (HIV-2) data/tooling are different?
Four checks, each answering a different version of the question:
(1) Is the rule engine faithful? A rule engine re-implemented from published Stanford HIV-2 penalty tables was applied to the clinical sequences and reproduced the HIVDB penalty exactly — R = 1.0000, 1,728/1,728 rows, 100% agreement. This proves the engine is faithful, and simultaneously proves the clinical target is a deterministic function of the mutation list (i.e. it is not an independent measurement).
(2) Are the two species genuinely different domains? HIV-1 (HXB2) and HIV-2 (ROD) differ at 51 of 99 positions — 48.5% identity. Despite that, the drug-binding pocket is conserved to 1.09 Å Cα RMSD, which is why pocket-local chemistry can transfer while global features cannot.
(3) Does transfer actually happen, or is it perfect/inert? The zero-shot transfer ratio is 0.699 (0.5163 / 0.7387) — substantial but clearly partial, confirming the domains are related but not the same.
(4) Does the European tool measure the same thing as our model? No. The official HIV-2EU v4 rules leave 83.1% of the measured panel unscored, define no protease rules for 6 of 8 clinical drugs, and cover only 5 of 37 observed mutations.
Where: §3.6 (check 1); §2.1 (check 2); §3.2 (check 3); §3.7 (check 4).

Reading of each row:
(I) SEQ works. Sequence-only physico­chemical descriptors carry genuine cross-species signal — R = 0.3582 in the zero-shot comparison (0.5163 in the locked 2,079-D space).
(II) STR alone fails across species. HIV-1-derived structural contact features give R = −0.1392 on HIV-2. This is the single clearest result in the modality analysis: HIV-1 geometry does not transfer even though HIV-1 chemistry does.
(IV) Naive SEQ+STR fusion is worse than SEQ alone — 0.2061 versus 0.3582. Simply concatenating a non-transferring modality with a transferring one degrades performance; the structural block injects noise the model cannot separate. This is the empirical justification for not fusing modalities, and it is why the deployment model is sequence-only.
(III) STR–LIGAND helps only when it is species-appropriate. Structural features computed on HIV-2 docked complexes — i.e. structure in the presence of the ligand, in the correct species — act as a useful residual-correction stage (corrected R = 0.6694, R² = 0.4385, n = 136). The contrast with row (II) is the whole point: the problem is not structure per se, it is structure borrowed from the wrong species.
Why (IV) would in principle be required — and what we found instead. A multimodal SEQ+STR+STR–LIGAND model is the natural design when each modality contributes complementary information. Here the modalities are not complementary across species: STR is actively harmful (−0.1392) and the naive fusion degrades the sequence signal. The correct species-matched use of structure is as a correction stage (III), not as a fused input. Our design follows that evidence.
Where: §3.3 and §4.4; Table 7; the docked-ligand poses are Figure 6.
D8Meta-learning / stack generalization — and what is the need?
Naming first: we use stacked generalization (stacking), not meta-learning. Meta-learning means learning across many tasks (MAML, prototypical networks). A meta-model is the second level of a stack. Same prefix, unrelated concepts — and the manuscript never claims meta-learning.
What we implemented. In StackingEnsemble: base (level-1) models are Ridge, SVR, RandomForest, XGBoost and DANN; the meta (level-2) model is a Ridge(alpha=5). The meta-model is fitted on the out-of-fold prediction matrix produced by 5-fold CV — one column per base model, each prediction made on data that model never saw.
Why stacking is needed — three concrete reasons.
(1) No single base model dominates. Across four drugs and several feature sets the best model changes. Committing to one model means committing to a model that is best on average and worst somewhere. Stacking lets a regularised learner weight the base models per problem instead of picking one.
(2) It measurably helped. The ridge meta-ensemble was the strongest zero-shot configuration at R = 0.5818, above XGBoost (0.5587), the simple RF+XGB ensemble (0.5467) and the global Random Forest (0.5163).
(3) Out-of-fold fitting prevents over-trust. If the meta-model were fitted on in-fold predictions, the base models would appear better than they are and the meta-model would over-weight them. Fitting on OOF predictions is what makes the 0.5818 trustworthy rather than optimistic.
The same idea appears in the deployment model, where the frozen HIV-1 prediction is appended as a single meta-feature (a stack with one base model), and a new per-drug Random Forest is the level-2 learner. Note for accuracy: DANN is domain adaptation (gradient-reversal alignment of source and target features), not meta-learning.
Where: §2.4 (protocol); §3.2 and Table 4 (results); §3.2 (why the meta-feature does not add measurably at n = 160).
